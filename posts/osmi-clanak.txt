Electrolux EEM48321L dishwasher constantly drawing water as soon as it's plugged in - how to fix it
4 May 2026

The Electrolux EEM48321L dishwasher (PNC 911 536 337 01, control board EDW-PB150 01E) started drawing water constantly from the moment the plug went into the socket. Whether a program was running or the machine was in stand-by, water flowed non-stop. The only way to stop it was to pull the cable from the wall. The repairman who first looked at the machine replaced two chips on the board, but the problem stayed. In the end I found the fault myself and repaired the machine - by replacing one 15 cent SMD capacitor and one 50 cent SMD triac, plus a new Aquastop valve. The whole repair came to about 70€, while a service center would charge 200-300€ for a new board alone.

## The symptom and the wrong diagnosis

A classic description of the problem: water flows **immediately** when the board is connected to power, before the processor even turns the system on. This rules out a software fault and points to a hardware fault in the circuit controlling the electromagnetic water inlet valve (Aquastop). The Aquastop is closed at rest and opens only when the microcontroller sends a signal to the triac.

The repairman replaced the TNY278GN (a Power Integrations power supply chip) and the CNY17-2 (an isolation optocoupler). The problem is that those chips are part of the board's power supply section - they generate 5V/12V for the logic. They have nothing to do with the circuit controlling the 230V triac for the valve. That's why the repair failed - the wrong part of the board was being fixed. I even tested with another, working valve - water still flowed constantly, which confirms the fault is on the board and not in the valve.

## The real cause – snubber capacitor C3065 and triac TY3001

After some research I came across the Polish forum elektroda.pl, where this fault is documented as a "popularna usterka" (common fault) of the PB150/PB200 modules. Several users described identical symptoms, and an experienced technician, AsReeX, solved the problem on multiple machines through private communication. The main suspects were two elements in the circuit controlling the Aquastop valve:

- **Triac TY3001** (Z0103MN, SOT-223 package, position S3037) - the electronic switch that turns 230V to the solenoid on and off
- **Snubber capacitor C3065** - a small 10nF/250V SMD ceramic capacitor next to the triac

![TY3001 controls the Aquastop](posts/8,2.jpg)

C3065 and resistor R3110 (47Ω) form an RC snubber circuit connected in parallel with the triac. A snubber is a protective component - its job is to absorb the voltage spikes that the inductive solenoid generates when the valve turns off. Without a snubber, those spikes can damage the triac.

When C3065 fails and becomes a short circuit (which happens after years of work on the 230V mains, especially close to the capacitor's maximum voltage of 250V), it creates a direct path for current that **bypasses the triac completely**. Current now flows: mains → valve solenoid → shorted C3065 → back to the mains. The triac has no control over the current. The microcontroller sends it the "turn off" signal, but that no longer matters because the current goes through the capacitor. The valve is under 230V continuously and water flows for as long as the machine is plugged in.

An additional problem is that when the snubber capacitor fails, the triac is exposed to the full voltage spikes and can also get damaged over time. That's why the forum recommendation is to replace **both** elements, which turned out to be right in my case.

## Secondary damage – the burnt blue wire

The direct result of the shorted C3065 is a thermal disaster at the solenoid valve connector. In normal operation, the solenoid turns on for 60-120 seconds per filling cycle. With a shorted C3065, the solenoid runs non-stop for hours, for days. The blue (neutral) wire at the valve connector was black, with soot deposits. This happens because of thermal runaway in the contact: I²R heating creates oxide, oxide increases resistance, higher resistance creates even more heating, and so on until micro-sparks appear and the plastic carbonizes. The original Aquastop valve had already been destroyed by prolonged overvoltage, so I had to replace it as well.

## Diagnostics with a multimeter

Before ordering parts, I wanted to check the components with a multimeter. I took the board out of the machine (after photographing all the connectors before unplugging them - a critical step so you know what goes where when putting it back) and tested all the suspicious elements:

- All 4 triacs (TY3001, TY3002, TY3004, TY3005) - all showed OL between MT1-MT2, which means they're not shorted
- Both HF3FA relays (RL3001, RL3002) - OK
- Optocouplers U2000 and U2002 (CNY17-2) - OK, LED shows 1042mV, phototransistor 640mV
- Diodes D3000 (SF 1600V), D3002, D3006 - all OK
- C3017 and the other large electrolytic capacitors - OK

What I only learned later is that **a micro crack on C3065 can't be detected with a multimeter**. A multimeter tests at 3V, while the capacitor only leaks at 230V. So all the diagnostic tests can show the capacitor is fine, while it only causes problems at working voltage. Similarly, a triac can test fine on a multimeter but only show the problem under load.

## Parts and tools

I ordered from TME.eu (delivery to Montenegro in 7 days, I had problems at customs):

- **Murata GRM31BR72J103KW01L** - ceramic capacitor 10nF/630V, SMD 1206 (replacement for C3065, with a higher voltage for more safety margin)
- **Littelfuse Z0103MNT1G** - triac 600V/1A, SOT-223 (replacement for TY3001)
- **Weller desoldering braid 1.5mm**

At Okov I bought:

- **Total 60W soldering iron** - the 15W one I had was too weak for a board with wide copper areas, the solder didn't melt even after 5 minutes of heating
- **Tinol wire** with a flux core
- **Kemper universal soldering paste** (for practice only - it's corrosive to electronics)

I also bought a **Temu test board** to practice soldering before touching the real board. I got a universal Aquastop valve with a hose from a local repairman.

## The repair procedure

I took the board out of the machine and photographed all the connectors. Then I focused on C3065 first. Desoldering the SMD capacitor took a couple of minutes - it's a small capacitor, and with a 60W iron and braid it comes off quickly. The technique I used: put a little paste on both ends, add fresh solder to one end (fresh solder melts at a lower temperature and helps melt the old one), then quickly move the iron left and right between the two ends until both melt at the same time, then gently push the component sideways with tweezers.

I put the board back in the machine, thought "that's it"... but it was still drawing water. The machine showed identical symptoms. That was the moment I realized the triac was damaged as well, probably from working for a long time without a functional snubber capacitor.

The second round of the repair was harder. The Z0103MN triac in a SOT-223 package has a large metal tab that carries heat away to a wide copper area on the board. That area pulls heat away faster than even a 60W iron can deliver it. It took a lot of paste, a lot of fresh solder and patience - holding the iron 5-10 seconds per pin, using braid to soak up the melted solder from the tab in several passes. When I finally got the old triac off, I soldered in the new Littelfuse one - carefully oriented according to the marks on the board, first tinned one pad, placed the component, soldered the first pin to fix it in place, and then the tab and the remaining pins.

## Replacing the Aquastop valve

The Aquastop valve is replaced through the machine's bottom panel. I followed the official Electrolux instructions I found on their UK site. You undo 4 PH2 screws on the bottom cover and 2 screws on the upper part of the left side panel, press 3 plastic clips at the back, then remove the left side. Only then can you reach the internal hose connection and the plastic clamp holding it.

The universal valve I bought had a different connector from the original (a smaller Molex type instead of large faston tabs), so I couldn't plug it in directly. The solution was to cut off the new connectors and solder the wires directly - blue to blue, the other pair to the other pair. Polarity doesn't matter because the solenoid is an AC component. I insulated the joints with heat-shrink tubing.

## The result

After putting everything back and connecting the new Aquastop valve, I did the first test with the **water tap on the wall closed**. I plugged the machine in and listened for the solenoid working or an attempt at filling. Silence. That was the proof the repair worked - with no shorted capacitor and with a new triac, the circuit again works normally under the microcontroller's control.

I opened the tap and started a program. The machine fills with water in a controlled way, drains in a controlled way, and everything works as it should.

## What I learned

The main lesson: when a repairman swears he's fixed something and the problem stays, he very likely repaired the wrong part of the board. Forums like elektroda.pl are gold for specific faults like this, because people there share solutions that don't exist anywhere else on the internet.

Second lesson: tools matter. I bought the 15W iron with good intentions, but for work on a real board with SMD components on copper areas you need at least 60W. 10€ more for a better iron saves hours of frustration.

Third lesson: in a snubber circuit, both elements (capacitor and triac) often die together. When one fails, the other is exposed to stress and fails soon after. If you're already touching the board, replace both straight away - a spare triac costs 50 cents.

And fourth: document everything as you work. Photographing connectors before unplugging them, keeping a template of how components were oriented on the board, noting which wire goes where - all of that prevents big problems when putting things back.

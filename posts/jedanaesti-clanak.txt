Rainfall nowcasting over Budva: fine-tuning the DGMR generative model and comparing it with pysteps methods
10 July 2026

Nowcasting is short-term precipitation forecasting over a range of zero to roughly ninety minutes. Numerical weather models don't help at that range because assimilation and initialization take more time than the forecast itself covers, so nowcasting relies on radar measurements and extrapolation. Over the last few months I tried something more ambitious than extrapolation for the Budva area: fine-tuning the DGMR generative model on the archive of a single radar. This text is a technical report on that attempt, with all the problems I ran into, and with the measurements that finally decided what goes into production.

## Data and preparation

The data source is the Uljenje radar and its archive from May 2023 to June 2026: 310,799 ODIM HDF5 files, of which 285,193 are usable .MAX. products (composite maximum reflectivity, 5 minute step). Each file is calibrated to Budva: the projection from the file is used to resample the native ~558 m grid to 256×256 pixels at 1 km resolution, with Budva (42.2864 N, 18.8400 E) at the center. Reflectivity is converted to rain rate with the Marshall-Palmer relation Z = 200 R^1.6, limited to [0, 128] mm/h.

A training sequence is a window of 22 consecutive frames with no gaps: 4 input frames (20 minutes of history) and 18 output frames (90 minutes of future). Out of 25,540 possible half-overlapping windows (11 frame shift), the filter keeps every one containing at least one rainy frame, plus 5% of dry windows as negative examples for calibration. Result: 10,612 sequences, about 30 GB in float16 format. The last 112 sequences, the most recent in time, are held out of training and serve only for evaluation.

## Generative adversarial networks (GAN)

A GAN consists of two neural networks trained at the same time, with opposing goals. The generator maps an input (here: past radar images plus a random latent vector) to a sample, and the discriminator judges whether a given sample is real or generated. The generator is optimized so that the discriminator can't tell its samples from real ones; the discriminator is optimized to tell them apart. At equilibrium the generator produces samples whose statistics are close to the real data.

For forecasting, one limitation that's easily overlooked in practice is important. The generator's goal is for samples to be realistic, not to be accurate at the level of an individual location. Realism and positional accuracy are different criteria and, as will be shown, often opposed.

## DGMR

DGMR (Deep Generative Model of Rainfall) was published by Ravuri and colleagues in Nature in 2021. They trained the model on the United Kingdom's radar network composite: 1.48 million sequences, 256×256 pixel crops at 1 km. The generator with 98 million parameters takes 4 past images and latent noise, and returns 18 future images. The loss function has three terms:

- a spatial discriminator judges the realism of individual images;
- a temporal discriminator judges the consistency of motion through the sequence;
- a grid-cell regularizer, a weighted L1 difference between the average of several generated samples and the real field.

![Diagram of the DGMR model: the generator takes input images and noise and produces 18 predictions; two discriminators judge realism, the grid-cell L1 term location accuracy](posts/11,1.png)

According to the paper itself, the discriminators without the grid-cell term give accuracy at the level of Eulerian persistence, a forecast that assumes the last observed image doesn't change. Positional accuracy comes exclusively from the grid-cell term. The cause lies in the nature of the criteria: the discriminator rewards sharp images with the spectrum of real radar and penalizes smoothing, while the L1 term rewards a prediction close to the conditional mean, which is smooth. The ratio of the weights of those two parts determines the model's behavior. For maximum positional accuracy the model is trained with a dominant or exclusive grid-cell term, which is the configuration used in this work.

## Fine-tuning: problems and solutions

The starting point is the OpenClimateFix PyTorch implementation (the skillful_nowcasting repository, package dgmr 1.4.3) with their published weights, trained on a small sample. Fine-tuning was done on a Colab A100 GPU, batch 16, with a Monte-Carlo average of 8 generated samples per step. Three problems marked the process.

The first is the output scale. The generator without an output activation gave values of the order of 10⁹ instead of millimeters per hour, so training spent thousands of steps on scaling instead of learning. A softplus activation on the output limits values to a non-negative range and removes the problem.

The second is the implementation of the grid-cell loss in the library itself. The GridCellLoss function sums absolute differences with an L1 norm instead of averaging them, and then, because of an operator precedence error, multiplies the result by 256×256/18 instead of dividing, so the loss reaches the order of 10¹¹. Independently of that, the weighting uses max(y+1, 24) instead of min: dry pixels get a weight of 24, the same as a downpour. The formula with max is in the Nature paper itself, while DeepMind's published pseudocode uses clip(y, 0, 24); a discussion of that mismatch exists in issue #331 of their repository. Training with such a loss converges towards suppressing rain. Replacing it with a correct, averaged and min-weighted version was a precondition for any progress.

The third is frequency bias, the ratio of forecast to observed rain area. The weighting of rainy pixels is a double-edged parameter: too little leads to suppressing rain (bias below 1), too much to overestimating the area (bias above 2). The solution is the parametrization w = 1 + k·min(y, 24), where the coefficient k is tuned according to the measured bias on the evaluation set, with the target bias ≈ 1. On top of that I introduced a rule that the best checkpoint is accepted only if its bias is in the range [0.7, 1.4], because the FSS metric rewards overestimating the area and without that limit picks poorly calibrated models.

## Evaluation centered on Budva

The entire evaluation is tied to the area the product actually covers. Metrics are computed on the central 128×128 km around Budva, on a frozen set of the 96 most recent held-out sequences, with fixed latent vectors for comparability between evaluations. There are two metrics:

- FSS (Fractions Skill Score) at a threshold of 1 mm/h, aggregated by components over all windows, at scales of 1, 5, 10 and 20 km, expressed as a ratio to persistence;
- the Brier score for the event "at least one pixel ≥ 1 mm/h within ±8 km of Budva", where the model's probability is the share of the 8 sample ensemble predicting the event.

The result of the fine-tuning: DGMR on this set beats persistence on both metrics. The best calibrated checkpoint reaches an FSS ratio of 1.02 and a Brier of 0.105 against 0.167 for persistence, at the +60 minute horizon. That is, as far as I know, a non-trivial result for one radar and a dataset of this size. The result, however, isn't stable: as training continues the metrics oscillate, the best states appear and vanish within spans of a few hundred steps, and training from the best state led to deterioration in two repeated attempts. Such behavior is expected for a model of 98 million parameters on 10 thousand sequences: DeepMind used a dataset two orders of magnitude larger for a stable result.

## Comparison with pysteps methods

For the production decision, what counts is a direct showdown with the methods the project already uses. The pysteps library does optical flow extrapolation: the Lucas-Kanade algorithm estimates a motion field from consecutive images, then the last observed field is advected forward. The ANVIL variant (Pulkkinen et al., 2020) adds an autoregressive model of rain growth and decay. Both methods are deterministic, require no training and run in seconds on a CPU.

The comparison was done on the identical 96 sequences, with identical metrics. Consistency check: the persistence Brier independently computed in this evaluation is 0.167, equal to the last digit to the value from the DGMR evaluation.

![Comparison of methods at +60 minutes: FSS ratio to persistence and Brier score for rain in Budva](posts/11,4.png)

At +90 minutes the advantage of the pysteps methods grows: the FSS ratio reaches 1.34 for extrapolation and 1.42 for ANVIL, with a Brier of 0.135 to 0.146 against 0.167 for persistence.

The conclusion of the comparison is unambiguous. ANVIL reaches an FSS ratio that DGMR doesn't reach even at its best moment, a Brier equal to the best DGMR checkpoint, and all of that stably, with no training, no GPU and not a single parameter to store. The reason is structural: at horizons of up to an hour, most of the achievable skill is the transport of existing rain, and advection literally carries the observed field over and can't lose its structure. A generative model has to reconstruct the whole field from a learned representation, and the advantage it could theoretically bring, learning the initiation and decay of cells, requires an amount of data one radar doesn't produce in three years.

## Current state in production

Production nowcasting in the budva-radar project stays on the pysteps library (radar/pysteps_nowcast.py): Lucas-Kanade optical flow, ANVIL for stratiform and LINDA for convective scenes. DGMR stays an experimental path (radar/dgmr_adapter.py), with the best checkpoint archived. The condition for changing that status is a result consistently better than the pysteps methods over several independent time periods, which isn't likely with the current amount of data. The archive grows by about 290 rainy sequences a month, so doubling the dataset is a matter of years; a more realistic path is pretraining on the European OPERA composite and then fine-tuning on Uljenje, if the need arises.

## Conclusions

1. The adversarial terms of a GAN produce realism, not positional accuracy. In DGMR the location accuracy comes from the grid-cell L1 regularizer; the discriminators alone give accuracy at the level of persistence.
2. Loss implementations should be verified before training. An error in the library (sum instead of mean, multiplying instead of dividing, max instead of min in the weight) led to days of convergence in the wrong direction.
3. The choice of metric determines the outcome of optimization. FSS rewards overestimating the rain area, Brier penalizes it; selection of the best model has to include a limit on frequency bias.
4. Evaluation has to measure what the product delivers. Metrics centered on Budva and the event "rain in Budva" gave a different picture from metrics over the whole domain.
5. DGMR on one radar can beat persistence, and that's a usable scientific result. Making that result stable requires an order of magnitude more data than three years of one archive contain.
6. For a single radar configuration, advection stays the strongest choice: pysteps ANVIL beats both persistence and the best DGMR, stably and with no training cost.
7. The amount of data is the main limit of the generative approach, not optimization. Stopping training at the right moment and keeping versioned checkpoints matter more than any hyperparameter.

Samsung SCX-4300 – the computer doesn't see it over USB and it pulls two sheets with Paper Jam 1: repairing the board and the solenoid
4 June 2026

The Samsung SCX-4300 (control board JC92-01762H) came to me with two completely independent faults that have nothing to do with each other. First: the computer didn't see the printer over USB at all — the data signal path on the board was physically destroyed, the pads of choke **L4** torn off and the copper traces broken. Second, which only showed up once USB worked: the printer would print the first sheet, immediately pull a SECOND blank one and report "Paper Jam 1" with a high-pitched whine. I fixed both faults **without buying a single part** — the USB with thin enameled wire (~0.1 mm) as a jumper to R137/R138, and the paper feed problem with a piece of insulating tape on the solenoid. Cost of parts: 0€. **Before this the printer had been at the "Zvis Computers" service in Budva, where they replaced the USB port but didn't remove the fault. The repairman said there had been a short circuit on the board and that it couldn't be repaired.**

![Samsung REVO SCX4200 main board (right), next to the device's main power board](posts/9,0.jpg)

## Two faults on one device

This printer is a good example of how one device can hide two separate faults that mask each other. As long as the computer didn't see the printer over USB, the paper feed problem couldn't even be noticed — there was no way to send a print job. Only once I solved the communication did the second, mechanical fault appear. That's why I solved them in that order: electronics first (USB), then mechanics (solenoid).

## First fault: destroyed USB signal path (choke L4)

The symptom was simple — Windows didn't register the printer at all when the USB cable was plugged in. No connect sound, no new device in Device Manager, nothing. The board's power worked (the printer turned on and went through initialization normally), so the problem had to be in the USB signal path itself and not in the power supply.

![The main board has a large number of connections to the printer's mechanical devices, from the gears to the smallest sensors](posts/9,3.jpg)

When I looked at the board under a magnifier, I saw damage around the USB-B connector. On this board the USB data lines (D+ and D−) don't go directly from the connector to the chip — they first pass through a common-mode choke **L4** that suppresses interference, then through two series resistors **R137** and **R138** before reaching the USB transceiver. The pads L4 was soldered to had been torn off the board, and the traces connecting them were broken. Part of that damage probably also spread through my earlier attempts — once a pad gives way, it easily goes further.

### Why the choke can be bypassed

The key thing to understand: common-mode choke L4 **isn't necessary for basic operation**. Its job is filtering interference (EMI), not carrying the useful signal. At USB Full Speed, over a short cable, the D+ and D− lines work without that choke. So instead of trying to restore the destroyed pads and traces of L4 (which is almost impossible once the pads are gone), the solution is to bypass that whole section: connect the data lines directly from the USB connector to a point right after the damage.

### The repair: enameled wire to R137/R138

The best anchor for a jumper is the through-hole pins of the USB-B connector itself — they're mechanically solid, soldered through the board, and don't move around like SMD pads. On the other side, the pads of resistors **R137** and **R138** are points that lead straight to the chip, past the destroyed L4 section. The jumper connects those two points and skips the damage.

I used thin enameled wire of about **0.1 mm** — the kind used for transformer windings. The procedure with **just a soldering iron (about 330°C, no hot air gun)**:

- Scrape and tin the ends of the enameled wire (the enamel is insulation — it has to come off with heat/scraping for the solder to take)
- Tin the matching USB-B connector pin and the resistor pad
- Solder one end of the wire to the connector pin, route it carefully to the resistor pad, then solder the other end
- Repeat the same for the other data line (one jumper for D+, another for D−)
- Check with a multimeter that there's no short between D+ and D−, or to ground/power

The standard USB-B pinout is: pin 1 = VBUS (+5V), pin 2 = D−, pin 3 = D+, pin 4 = ground. Which line goes to R137 and which to R138 is easiest to confirm with a multimeter (a continuity check from the resistor pad towards the chip/connector) before soldering.

Important: the two jumpers must not touch each other or short against the surrounding traces. Enameled wire is ideal for exactly that reason — it's thin, flexible, and where the enamel stays unscraped it adds insulation. With 0.1 mm wire and a fine iron tip this can be done precisely and without hot air.

![View of the top side of the board. Two enameled copper wires connect to the D+ and D- rails respectively](posts/9,1.jpg)

![View of the bottom side of the board. Two enameled copper wires start from pins 2 and 3 of the port itself, which is soldered to the board. Those pins carry the data on the full-speed USB data rail](posts/9,2.jpg)

After this, the computer recognized the printer right away. **USB connection fixed.**

## Second fault: double paper feed and "Paper Jam 1"

Only once USB worked and I could send print jobs did the second fault come out. The printer would:

1. print the first sheet and almost eject it,
2. immediately pull a SECOND blank sheet,
3. report "Paper Jam 1" with a high-pitched whine.

This turns out to be the most documented fault on exactly this engine — the SCX-4300 shares its engine with the SCX-4200 and the whole family (ML-1510/1710, SCX-4100/4200/4216, Xerox PE114/Phaser 3119/WC 3119...). The "Jam 1" logic is simple: after a sheet activates the feed (registration) sensor, the exit sensor has to change state within a set time window; if that doesn't happen — or if both sensors stay active too long — the firmware reports a jam. Pulling a second sheet while the drive is still running is a classic sign that the **one-revolution pickup clutch isn't returning to its home position** and the machine grabs a new sheet.

### Engine Test Mode

To check this, I went into Engine Test Mode. On the SCX-4300 (identical to the SCX-4200) the sequence, pressed quickly one after another, is:

**Menu → Copies → ◄ (left) → ► (right) → Menu → ◄ (left)**

The screen briefly shows "Engine Test [Diagnostic]". Start/OK enters the test, Menu scrolls the list, Stop exits. The pickup test is in the section **MTR FAN SOL ETC → PickUp Test**, and the motor separately in **Motor Test**.

### A strange diagnosis: no click until I move the cam

This was the most interesting part, and it almost led me down the wrong path. When I run PickUp Test from rest — **nothing, no solenoid click at all**. But when I run Motor Test, I can see the gray roller/cam turn and stop somewhere **in the middle** of the cycle, not at the home position. And only then, when I turn off Motor Test and immediately turn on PickUp Test — **only then is there a click**.

That first made me suspect the metal frame the board sits on, which I had taken apart and put back earlier — that maybe the gears weren't meshing well after reassembly. But the explanation was different and more precise.

### The mechanism: one-revolution clutch and a stuck tab

The pickup mechanism works like this: the clutch has a tab/armature held by the solenoid. At rest the tab sits in a notch of the cam and keeps it "parked". When the processor sends a pulse (~300 ms, 24V), the solenoid pulls the tab, the clutch engages the drive and makes exactly **one revolution**, then the tab drops back into the notch and stops it.

When the cam stops outside its true home position (as in Motor Test), a latch tooth **presses on the armature and mechanically blocks it** — the solenoid is powered, but the armature physically has nowhere to go, so there's no click. As soon as the cam turns a couple of degrees further, the armature is released and clicks. That means one important thing: **the solenoid coil and the driver on the board WORK** — the proof is that it clicks as soon as it's released. The problem is purely mechanical, not electronic, and has nothing to do with the board.

The real cause in my case: on the end face of the solenoid (between the core and the armature) there's a small sponge/rubber pad whose job is to stop the armature from magnetically sticking to the core. That pad had deteriorated over time and turned sticky, so **the armature (tab) was sticking to the solenoid** and not releasing cleanly. Because of that the clutch didn't return home properly, and from there came the double feed and Jam 1.

### The repair: cleaning and insulating tape

The solution is as cheap as it gets:

- I removed the remains of the old sticky pad
- Cleaned both the core and the metal armature (tab) of all adhesive residue (IPA)
- Stuck a small piece of **insulating tape** on the end face as a new, non-stick pad

Important: you must not leave bare metal on metal. The surfaces are ground so finely that even the slightest residual magnetism or traces of grease hold the armature when the solenoid has no power — so it knocks and sticks again. A piece of tape gives just the right gap for the armature to separate instantly. (Kapton tape lasts longer than regular insulating tape, but regular tape works well too.)

After the tape — the click is clean from rest, one revolution, one sheet per command. Then I ran 5 pages in a row to confirm the double feed doesn't come back. It works.

## Parts and tools

The nicest thing about this repair: **I didn't buy a single part.** Both faults were solved with material I have on the desk anyway.

- **Thin enameled wire ~0.1 mm** — for the USB jumpers (winding wire, the kind for transformers)
- **Insulating tape** (or Kapton) — for the non-stick solenoid pad
- **Soldering iron ~330°C, fine tip** — no hot air gun; the whole USB repair was done with just an iron
- **IPA (isopropyl alcohol)** — for cleaning the solenoid core and armature
- **Magnifier and tweezers** — a must for work around the USB pads
- **Multimeter** — for checking for shorts on the USB lines after soldering

If cleaning and tape don't help in your case, the replacement parts for the mechanics are: pickup solenoid **JC33-00009A** (SOLENOID-HB PICK-UP, 24V), clutch/cam **JC66-00377A** / **MEA UNIT-CLUTCH JC97-01788A**, return spring **6107-001170**. The manual solenoid (for manual feed) is **JC33-00010A** — don't confuse it with the pickup one; when it sticks you see it as "prints only from halfway down the page" from the manual feed.

## The result

The printer now: the computer sees it right away over USB, it prints normally, and it picks exactly one sheet per command — no Jam 1 and no whine. Two faults, two approaches (electronics then mechanics), zero parts bought.

## What I learned

1. **One device can hide two faults.** While USB was dead, the mechanical feed problem couldn't be seen at all. First solve what's blocking your testing (communication), then whatever shows up after that.
2. **You can sometimes bypass a filter component.** Common-mode choke L4 isn't there to carry signal but to deal with interference. When its pads and traces are destroyed, a thin wire jumper straight to R137/R138 brings USB back. It isn't as "pretty" as the original trace, but it works and avoids hopeless patching of torn pads.
3. **Thin enameled wire and just an iron are enough for fine jumpers.** The enamel also serves as insulation; 0.1 mm wire routes easily with no short and no need for a hot air gun.
4. **"No click" doesn't always mean a dead solenoid.** If the solenoid clicks as soon as you move the mechanism by hand (or with Motor Test), the coil and the board work — the armature is just mechanically blocked in the home position. That's mechanics, not electronics, and it isn't the main board.
5. **The cheapest part is often the solution.** A piece of insulating tape fixed a fault someone would have replaced as a "faulty pickup solenoid" or even worse — "main board". A sponge pad turned to glue is a classic on exactly this engine.
6. **Engine Test Mode is gold for diagnosis.** Instead of guessing, PickUp Test and Motor Test showed me exactly that the problem was in the clutch returning, not in a sensor or the board — and saved hours of removing the wrong parts.

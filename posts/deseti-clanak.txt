The famous "bumpgate" scandal: How NVIDIA made 20 million defective chips
23 June 2026

If your laptop, desktop or PlayStation 3 or Xbox 360 console made somewhere between 2007 and 2009 died by having the picture fall apart first (artifacts, colored lines, "snow" and squares across the screen) and then one day the screen stayed completely black even though the machine still turned on and the fan spun, there's a good chance you didn't have bad luck, but a **defective chip**. And not just any chip: this is one of the biggest hardware scandals in the history of consumer electronics, known by the nickname **"bumpgate"**. According to estimates circulating at the time, NVIDIA shipped about **20 million** graphics chips and chipsets between 2006 and 2008 with a built-in flaw that, after two to three years of perfectly normal operation, almost certainly killed them. I'm currently working on a machine with exactly this flaw — a Toshiba Satellite X200 with a GeForce 8700M GT card, whose G84 chip (made in week 27 of 2007) is a textbook bumpgate candidate. So it seemed like a good moment to explain what actually happened, because the story is darker and more technically interesting than it looks at first glance.

![GeForce 8700M GT on an MXM card from the Toshiba — the G84 chip in the middle is a classic bumpgate specimen](posts/10,0.jpg)

## What is "bumpgate" anyway

The name comes from the English word *bump* — the tiny solder balls that connect the silicon die to the chip's substrate from the inside. The scandal got its name when it became clear that those very balls were cracking. But to understand why, we first need to see how the fault shows up, because the pattern of failure is exactly what makes it so insidious.

A typical scenario looks like this: the chip works flawlessly for a year, two, sometimes three. Then the first symptoms appear. Occasional artifacts on the screen, brief picture freezes, "garbage" pixels. As time passes the fault gets worse: the picture keeps deteriorating, until it ends in complete failure. A black screen, no picture, while everything else (power, fan, disk) still runs. For some it was a gradual process, for others a sudden death.

Every G84 chip from that period carries the same flaw — the only question is when it will show, and that depends almost entirely on how hot the chip ran during its life. That's why poorly cooled laptops died within a year, while better cooled ones lasted four or five. The first public voices about the problem only appeared in September 2008, even though the chips had been in production since 2006; it simply took time for them to start failing en masse and for the pattern to be noticed.

## Anatomy of a chip: die, bumps, substrate and what's on the outside

For the next part to make sense, we have to separate two things that constantly get mixed up in practice.

A modern graphics chip is a so-called **flip-chip** assembly. The silicon die isn't wire-bonded to the substrate; instead several hundred, even over a thousand, tiny solder balls are placed on its underside — those are the **bumps**. The die is then turned upside down (hence "flip") and lowered on those balls and soldered directly to a ceramic or organic substrate. The space between the die and the substrate is then filled with epoxy resin — **underfill** — which protects the balls from moisture and dirt but, more importantly, reinforces them mechanically and takes part of the stress on itself.

Only below that substrate, on the underside of the whole chip, are the BGA balls, the larger, visible ones that connect the finished chip to the laptop's motherboard.

Here's why this difference is crucial: **bumpgate is a failure of the inner balls (bumps) between the die and the substrate, and NOT of the outer BGA balls.**

![Simplified cross-section of a flip-chip: the die on top, below it a row of bumps embedded in underfill, then the substrate, then the BGA balls towards the motherboard. Bumpgate cracks in the top row, not the bottom one](posts/10,1.jpg)

## The real cause: the wrong combination of materials

NVIDIA itself, when it finally had to admit the problem, used the vague wording **"weak die/packaging material set"**. Translated from corporate to human, that roughly means: *"we messed up both the alloy and the glue, but we'll wrap it in four learned English words so it sounds like higher mathematics and not an ordinary defect."* Behind that euphemistic phrase hides a combination of several bad decisions that added up to a disaster.

The first ingredient is the balls themselves. For the bumps NVIDIA used a high-lead alloy, while the contact surfaces on the substrate were eutectic tin. Such a "composite" joint (a high-lead ball on a eutectic surface) is mechanically weaker and more sensitive to material fatigue than a pure eutectic joint. A common misconception is worth breaking here: many people immediately blamed bumpgate on lead-free solder and the RoHS directive that imposed it in 2006. The truth is the opposite and more subtle. The balls were in fact leaded, and the problem was in that unfortunate combination of alloys and in what follows.

The second ingredient is the underfill. NVIDIA used a material labeled Namics 8439-1, whose mechanical properties (stiffness and softening temperature, the so-called Tg) didn't match what those hot chips demanded. Instead of giving the balls enough mechanical support and "relief" during expansion, the underfill left them exposed.

The third and decisive ingredient is heat. G84 dies had pronounced hotspots, and a laptop as an enclosure doesn't allow the luxury of a big heatsink. Every time a chip heats up and cools down — which happens with every power on, power off and gaming load — the materials expand and contract at different rates (the die, the balls and the substrate have different coefficients of thermal expansion). That stress, repeated thousands of times through thermal cycles, slowly creates and spreads micro-cracks in the balls until they finally lose contact.

## Why reflow and reball only help temporarily (and often don't help)

This is the part every repair technician has to understand, because it's surrounded by the most misconceptions and wasted money.

**Reflow** is a procedure in which the whole chip is heated to the melting temperature of solder. The heat briefly softens the cracked inner balls and "moves" them back into contact, so the chip works again. It sounds like a repair — but it isn't. The root of the problem (bad alloy, bad underfill, hotspots) stays untouched, so the balls crack again, most often within a couple of weeks to a couple of months. Each following reflow lasts shorter and shorter, because the material keeps degrading. That's why everyone who reflowed an Xbox 360, PS3 or one of these laptops noticed that the fault always "pops up" again.

**Reball** is a more serious procedure: the chip is removed, its old **BGA balls** are stripped, new ones are applied and the chip is soldered back onto the board. The problem? A reball replaces the **outer** balls — and with bumpgate those are usually not faulty at all. The real fault is in the **inner** bumps, which a reball never reaches. The little improvement a reball does bring comes only from the fact that heating the chip, as a side effect, also reflows those inner balls. In other words, you reball the wrong balls, and the repair you get is actually an accidental reflow of the right ones. Temporarily.

All of this leads to an unpleasant conclusion: the only permanent solution is replacing the chip with a fixed revision. Everything else is buying time.

## How to recognize a fixed revision

During the summer of 2008 NVIDIA began producing fixed versions of the chips — mainly by **replacing the Namics 8439-1 underfill with Hitachi's 3730 material**, which gave the balls much better mechanical support. Luckily for those of us repairing these, the good revisions can be recognized with the naked eye and by their marking:

- **Underfill color.** Defective chips have **dark (black) underfill** around the edge of the die; fixed ones have **white/light** underfill. This is the quickest visual test.
- **Datecode.** Good revisions carry a production date **after 0830** (week 30 of 2008) — though you should be careful, because some defective units were produced a bit after that date as well.
- **Odd digit in the marking.** The most reliable sign: NVIDIA often marked fixed versions with an **odd instead of an even digit** at the end of the model number. A classic example is G84-603-A2 (good) versus G84-602-A2 (defective). For my specific case — the 8700M GT — that's exactly the difference between **G84-750-A2 (defective)** and **G84-751-A2 (fixed)**, and that's why for the Toshiba I'm looking for a unit with the "-751" marking, white underfill and a date from 2009–2010.

A small but important caveat: even the "fixed" revision wasn't a perfect cure. NVIDIA replaced the underfill, but **it didn't change the alloy of the balls themselves** until the Fermi generation (GF100). In other words, they fixed the easier thing (the glue) and left the harder part of the job (the alloy) for some better day — truly in the spirit of that old folk wisdom that a job should first be started with enthusiasm, and then one should rest nicely until the need for it passes on its own. And for the irony to be complete, when they finally sorted out that alloy on Fermi, some cards (GTX 470/480) went back to that same bad underfill — so they were fixing with one hand what they had just broken with the other. That's why even good revisions, if you keep them at high temperatures, live longer but not forever — and cooling is still decisive.

## The scale of the scandal: money, lawsuits and silence

NVIDIA never officially confirmed the figure of 20 million chips, nor did it publish an exact number of units. But knowing that **all** G84 and G86 chips were affected, and that they were used by almost every major manufacturer, estimates of tens of millions of units aren't exaggerated.

The financial hit was huge and grew for years:

- In July 2008 NVIDIA stated in a filing with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) a reserve of **150 to 200 million dollars** for warranties, repairs and replacements.
- The company's stock fell about **25%** when the news came out.
- The final, cumulative cost ended up at **475.9 million dollars** — more than double the initial estimate, because failures kept coming from more and more models that NVIDIA at first didn't even consider at risk.

Lawsuits followed. Owners of **HP, Dell and Apple** laptops joined class action lawsuits. The settlement included a fund of about **2 million dollars** to reimburse repair costs, plus about **13 million** in attorney fees that NVIDIA took on — with, of course, the standard wording that it "admits no wrongdoing". Manufacturers extended warranties on the affected models, and some (Dell, HP) meanwhile released BIOS patches that forced the fan to spin more often — which did delay the chip's death, but at the cost of noisier operation and shorter battery life.

The list of affected devices is long and well known: Apple **MacBook Pro 15" and 17"** (8600M GT, 2007–2008), Dell **XPS M1330 and M1530**, the **Inspiron** and **Latitude** series, HP **Pavilion dv2000/dv6000/dv9000**, various **Acer Aspire**, **Sony VAIO**, certain **Lenovo** and **Fujitsu Siemens** models, **Asus**... Practically a whole generation of mid-range laptops.

And perhaps the worst — and, let's admit it, the most entertaining — part of the whole story was the way NVIDIA behaved. Instead of a simple "we made a mistake, here's the solution", the company played the complete crisis management repertoire: first silence, then denial, then shifting the blame. And as the months went by, it dumped the blame on everyone in turn except itself — first on HP (their batch, their problem), then on material suppliers, then on laptop manufacturers (they cooled the chip badly), and finally, in the SEC filing, mildly on the customers themselves. Yes, on the customers. Roughly along the logic that this defective chip would have worked flawlessly for you if you hadn't had that inconvenient habit of turning on and using your computer.

If NVIDIA had had its own **commandments of crisis management** in those years, carved in marble at the entrance to its headquarters, they would have read something like this:

1. Admit nothing you can keep quiet about.
2. Don't keep quiet about what you can dump on a supplier.
3. If you really must admit it, admit it in English — and in four words no living soul understands.
4. Repair quietly, and swear loudly there was nothing to repair.
5. Extend the warranty just enough for the fault to pop up the day after it expires.
6. The guilty party is always the next one in the chain — supplier, assembler, customer, air humidity. Just not you.

Jokes aside (and honestly, it's barely a joke), that's mostly how it played out. The "limited batch" story was held onto stubbornly, even though independent sources — above all the journalist Charlie Demerjian and the site SemiAccurate — used an electron microscope to show that **all** chips of that family were affected. The most vivid proof was a series of **"Product Change Notice"** documents with which NVIDIA quietly changed the underfill on the G86, G92 and others, while those same documents stated with a straight face that the change was made "to improve package robustness" — not at all, God forbid, because of some kind of defect. It turns out they were changing perfectly good chips, just like that, out of pure love of modification.

And here we come to a detail that especially warms the hearts of us from this part of the world: the settlement mentioned above applied to buyers in the United States. Owners of those same laptops in Europe — including, say, one repairman in Montenegro with a Toshiba full of G84 chips on his workbench — got exactly what a Montenegrin, according to his own mythology, knows how to receive with the most dignity: nothing. But nothing with pride. While Americans sent their laptops in for free replacement, we on this side of the ocean waved at the plane — and then, well, picked up the soldering iron ourselves. Which, to be fair, isn't exactly the worst possible outcome.

## The bigger picture: Xbox 360, PS3 and the lead-free solder era

Bumpgate wasn't an isolated incident, but the best known representative of a whole epidemic of BGA failures from the late 2000s. From the same period and the same class of problems are:

- **Xbox 360 "Red Ring of Death"**, where the ATI/TSMC graphics chip died in a very similar way (Microsoft never fully explained it technically, but it extended warranties to three years and spent billions).
- **PlayStation 3 "fat" YLOD (Yellow Light of Death)**, where the RSX Reality Synthesizer graphics chip (based on NVIDIA architecture) cracks on the same principle. I've done quite a bit of PS3 reballing myself in the past, so this whole story is very familiar to me first-hand.

Those years also brought the switch to **lead-free solder** (RoHS, 2006), which undoubtedly complicated things — lead-free alloys melt at a higher temperature and are more brittle. But, as I already stressed, in bumpgate itself the balls were leaded; lead-free solder is part of the wider context of the era, and not a direct culprit for this particular fault. What connects all these cases is the same pattern: a hot chip + insufficient cooling + repeated thermal cycles = fatigue and cracking of BGA/bump joints.

## Conclusions

1. **A material defect isn't visible right away.** These chips worked for years before they started to fail. The most dangerous flaws are exactly the ones that only show after thousands of working cycles, long after the warranty has expired.
2. **Reflow isn't a permanent repair, and reball often doesn't touch the real problem.** In bumpgate the inner (die-to-substrate) balls crack, not the outer BGA ones. Reflow briefly moves them back, reball replaces the wrong balls — both are buying time.
3. **The only permanent solution is replacing the chip with a fixed revision** — white underfill, datecode after 0830, odd digit in the marking (in my case: G84-751 instead of G84-750).
4. **Cooling is decisive.** A chip that never goes above 55–60°C can live a very long time even if it's defective. Half the repair is good paste, a clean heatsink and a working fan.
5. **Manufacturers stay silent for as long as the problem is within the warranty period.** Bumpgate, RROD and YLOD are textbook examples of how a big company behaves when it discovers a mass defect — first denial, then shifting the blame, then quiet settlements. And of course, only where the courts can reach.
6. **Forums and independent sources are gold.** Most of the technical truth about bumpgate didn't come from NVIDIA, but from persistent journalists and repair technicians who took chips apart and published their findings. Without them the whole story would still be a "limited batch" today.

To close, the Toshiba on my desk is living proof that bumpgate, almost two decades later, still arrives on the workbench. The chip is waiting for its "-751" replacement, and I'll write about the transplant procedure itself (stencil, balls, hot air) separately when the parts arrive.
